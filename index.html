What “half-life” means here

Evidence half-life is a decision aid, not a scientific constant. It is the point at which an observation should carry roughly half its original confidence because customers, workflows, positioning, pricing, and alternatives may have changed. A recent direct interview about a current workflow can remain useful for months. A second-hand note from before a major product launch may be stale almost immediately.

The calculator starts with a simple cadence-based baseline. Teams that collect evidence every week get a shorter default half-life because they can detect change quickly. Quarterly programs receive a longer baseline because they work with a slower review rhythm. The change setting then shortens or lengthens that window. This deliberately favors caution: the cost of a short revalidation check is usually lower than the cost of building against an obsolete assumption.

How to use the result

75–100%: current enough to use

The evidence is recent relative to your operating cadence. It can support prioritization, but it still needs normal checks for sample quality, customer relevance, and contradictory evidence. Freshness does not repair a weak source.

40–74%: use with a revalidation plan

The finding may still be directionally right, but a decision should include a quick confirmation step. Revisit two or three recent conversations, check whether the same friction appears in support or sales material, and record what changed.

Below 40%: treat it as a hypothesis

Do not present the old finding as current fact. Re-open the question, collect recent examples, and look specifically for disconfirming evidence. If the roadmap item is expensive or difficult to reverse, require stronger validation before commitment.

A lightweight revalidation workflow

  1. Name the exact claim. Replace broad statements such as “onboarding is confusing” with a testable observation: who struggles, during which task, and what outcome is blocked.
  2. Mark the evidence boundary. Record the customer segment, product version, source dates, number of examples, and whether the evidence was direct or summarized by someone else.
  3. Search recent material first. Review current conversations, tickets, calls, surveys, and win-loss notes for the same behavior. A useful customer feedback analysis process keeps the source evidence connected to the product decision instead of reducing it to vote counts.
  4. Look for contradiction. Ask what would prove the claim wrong. A newer workflow, changed packaging, a different buyer, or a solved integration gap can invalidate an otherwise well-supported finding.
  5. Update confidence visibly. Write “confirmed,” “narrowed,” “changed,” or “not found” with the review date. Preserve the old record so future readers can see why the decision moved.

Choose the right review cadence

High-change evidence deserves shorter intervals. Review findings sooner when the product area is being redesigned, when a new competitor changes customer expectations, when pricing or packaging moves, or when the evidence comes from prospects rather than active customers. Stable compliance workflows and deeply embedded operational habits may age more slowly, but they should still be checked after major organizational or regulatory change.

A practical rule is to attach the next review date when evidence first enters a roadmap document. That is easier than auditing an entire backlog later. For important bets, also assign an owner and a clear trigger—for example, “revalidate after the new admin experience reaches general availability.”

Limits of the score

The percentage measures time-based decay only. It does not measure truth, representativeness, commercial value, urgency, or implementation risk. Ten recent comments from the wrong segment can be less useful than two older observations from the customers affected by the decision. Use the score to prompt a review, never to automate a roadmap decision.

Keep the date of evidence separate from the date of interpretation. A newly written summary based on year-old conversations still contains year-old evidence.

The method is advisory and intentionally simple.
